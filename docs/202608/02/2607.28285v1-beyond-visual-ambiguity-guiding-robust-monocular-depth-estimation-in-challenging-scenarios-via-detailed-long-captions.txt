## 1 Introduction 

Monocular depth estimation (MDE) aims to predict a dense depth map from a single image, which is critical for applications like novel view synthesis [ 19 ], bokeh rendering [ 22 ], and virtual reality [ 28 ]. However, its acknowledged ill-posed and under-constrained nature makes MDE susceptible to the visual ambiguities in challenging scenarios [ 5, 24 , 26 , 32 , 41 ]. In particular, non-Lambertian surfaces (e.g. , glass or mirror) introduce the texture ambiguity [ 25 ], while adverse weather conditions ( e.g. , rain or night) cause the visibility ambiguity [ 2 ]. While general MDE methods [ 8, 9, 11 , 13 , 35 , 36 , 38 ], such as Depth Anything [ 35 ], achieve impressive MDE performance, they still struggle with the visual ambiguities, leading to degraded results in both challenging scenarios as illustrated in Fig. 1 (a). To improve robustness in challenging scenarios, some disjointed methods [ 5, 32 ] rely on scenario-specific data augmentation strate-gies for robust MDE as shown in Fig. 1 (b). For example, Depth4ToM [ 5] employs the semantic segmentation model [ 37 ] to segment transparent or mirror (ToM) surfaces and inpaints them with uni-form colors. RobustDepth [ 32 ] uses a physics-based renderer to synthesize rain and fog augmentations in driving scenes. Despite marginal improvements, their scenario-specific nature makes them inapplicable to other challenging scenarios. This naturally raises an intriguing question: Is it possible to develop a unified framework that simultaneously alleviates the visual ambiguities arising from both non-Lambertian surfaces and adverse weather conditions? 

To answer this question, we explore language, a powerful com-plementary modality that provides explicit guidance for scene un-derstanding [ 3 , 6, 16 , 20 , 21 ]. Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) [ 3, 40 ] further demonstrate that detailed long cap-tions, rather than simple short texts, significantly enhance visual perception capabilities in tasks such as object localization and seg-mentation [ 3, 6, 16 , 20 , 21 ]. This suggests that detailed long captions could potentially guide MDE in alleviating visual ambiguities in challenging scenarios, achieving enhanced robustness. For example, in the nighttime driving scene (Fig. 1), a detailed long caption input containing a sentence like “A vehicle is in front of and to the right of the trees.” provides richer spatial clues than a brief text input like “a photo of a car” [ 42 ]. Such language guidance could effec-tively alleviate visibility ambiguities, enabling robust MDE both on non-Lambertian surfaces and under adverse weather conditions. To harness such rich language guidance, three key questions naturally arise: (1) What formal template should the input text follow? (2) How to extract effective text features to guide MDE? (3) How can text features effectively guide depth prediction? Prior language-integrated MDE methods [ 39 , 42 ] provide suboptimal solutions. Specifically, they employ simple short text input tem-plates with limited information ( e.g. , “a photo of a <class name>” [ 42 ]). Moreover, they extract coarse global text features ( e.g. , the [CLS] token [ 39 , 42 ]) and rely on features that exhibit a gap from the precise geometric reasoning required for MDE [ 15 ] ( e.g. , U-Net noise prediction features [ 42 ]) for depth decoding without language guidance. Consequently, they fail to fully exploit the potential of language to alleviate visual ambiguities as shown in Fig. 1 (c). To better address the three questions, we propose CapDepth (Fig. 1 (d)), a robust MDE framework that leverages detailed long captions to guide the alleviation of visual ambiguities in challeng-ing scenarios (non-Lambertian surfaces and adverse weather con-ditions). Correspondingly, CapDepth consists of three key com-ponents: (1) a detailed long caption input template composed of spatially descriptive sentences, which explicitly conveys rich spa-tial information to guide MDE, (2) a dynamic caption encoder that captures fine-grained depth-relevant text features via progressive masked attention, (3) a text-adaptive decoder that leverages stable adaptive layer normalization to bridge the aforementioned feature gap, guiding enhanced depth decoding with text features. We conduct extensive experiments on established benchmarks, including Booster [ 25 ], ClearGrasp [ 31 ], nuScenes [ 2 ], and Driv-ingStereo [ 34 ]. Comprehensive comparisons are performed against prevalent general MDE methods [ 8, 9, 11 , 13 , 35 , 36 , 38 ], prior ro-bust MDE approaches [ 5 , 32 ], and existing language-integrated MDE models [ 39 , 42 ]. Results demonstrate that CapDepth effec-tively leverages guidance from detailed long captions to alleviate visual ambiguities arising from both non-Lambertian surfaces and adverse weather conditions. Consequently, CapDepth significantly improves MDE robustness in challenging scenarios, achieving depth error reductions of 25.0% and 22.0% in the respective scenarios. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows: 

• A detailed long caption input template that explicitly con-veys rich spatial relationships via multiple atom sentences to provide language guidance. 

• A dynamic caption encoder that extracts fine-grained depth-relevant text features from detailed long captions via pro-gressive masked attention. 

• A text-adaptive decoder that leverages stable adaptive layer normalization to guide the enhanced depth decoding process with the extracted text features.

## 2.2 Robust MDE 

To achieve robust MDE on non-Lambertian surfaces and under adverse weather conditions, recent efforts [5, 32] address them in-dependently. Specifically, Depth4ToM [ 5] inpaints segmented trans-parent or mirror (ToM) surfaces with uniform colors, while Robust-Depth [ 32 ] synthesizes weather effects via physics-based rendering. However, such augmentations introduce a domain gap between synthesized images and the real world, and weather augmentation apparently cannot handle non-Lambertian surfaces, and vice versa. In contrast, our CapDepth leverages detailed long captions as a unified guidance to simultaneously alleviate visual ambiguities and enhance robustness across both challenging scenarios. 

## 2.3 Language-Integrated MDE 

Recent MDE studies explore incorporating text [ 39 , 42 ]. For in-stance, extracting [CLS] token from simple short text, VPD [ 42 ]uses noise prediction features for depth decoding, while WorDepth [39 ] uses conditional sampling features. However, they exhibit three limitations: (1) simple short text input with limited information; (2) coarse global text feature extraction; (3) relying on features that exhibit a gap from precise geometric reasoning required for MDE [ 15 ]. In contrast, our CapDepth addresses these with: (1) a detailed long caption input template providing rich spatial clues; (2) a dynamic caption encoder extracting fine-grained depth-relevant text features; (3) a text-adaptive decoder leveraging text features to bridge the feature gap and guide enhanced depth decoding. 

## 3 Method 

In this section, we first provide an overview in Sec. 3.1. Then we elaborate on the detailed long caption input template in Sec. 3.2. The dynamic caption encoder will be discussed in Sec. 3.3. The text-adaptive decoder is described in Sec. 3.4. 

## 3.1 Overview 

Here we present an overview of our CapDepth framework. Given a single image 𝐼 ∈ R𝐻 ×𝑊 ×3 and its corresponding caption 𝐶 as inputs, our goal is to predict a dense depth map 𝐷 ∈ R𝐻 ×𝑊 . 𝐻 and 

𝑊 represent the height and width of the image. As discussed in Sec. 1, the keys to the exploitation of language can be divided into three parts: (1) text input template; (2) text feature extraction; and (3) text guidance on depth decoding. Accordingly, as depicted in Fig. 2, CapDepth proposes (1) a detailed long caption input template that MM ’26, November 10–14, 2026, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil Junrui Zhang, Jiaqi Li, Yiran Wang, Liao Shen and Zhiguo Cao 

provides rich spatial information; (2) a dynamic caption encoder for fine-grained depth-relevant text feature learning; and (3) a text-adaptive decoder for an enhanced depth decoding. First, as discussed in Sec. 1, to guide MDE in challenging scenar-ios with visual ambiguities, the language modality needs to convey rich spatial information. To this end, we propose a detailed long caption input template, which consists of many structured atom sentences that concisely convey such spatial information. Subse-quent analysis and experiments confirm the efficacy of this template over previous simple short text input templates [39, 42]. Second, although the input caption contains rich spatial infor-mation, extracting coarse global features ( e.g. , the [CLS] token) as in prior works [ 39 , 42 ] inevitably discards the valuable guidance provided by the language modality. To address this, we introduce a dynamic caption encoder that learns to extract fine-grained text features via progressive masked querying blocks. Concurrently, a learnable soft mask is employed to dynamically distinguish depth-relevant tokens for guiding robust MDE. Finally, text features and image features first enter the diffusion U-Net [ 30 ] for initial interaction, obtaining the internal U-Net repre-sentations. However, these representations are originally optimized for noise prediction [ 30 ], which creates a gap between the denoising objective and the precise geometric reasoning required for MDE [15 ]. To bridge this gap and fully exploit language guidance during depth decoding, we propose a text-adaptive decoder. Specifically, it leverages the text features to modulate the visual features via sta-ble adaptive layer normalization (SAdaLN). By explicitly injecting these text-based spatial clues into the depth decoding process, our decoder effectively aligns the visual features with the MDE task, thereby providing strong guidance for more accurate and robust depth predictions in challenging scenarios. 

## 3.2 Detailed Long Caption Input Template 

As discussed in Sec. 1, recent works [ 3 , 40 ] demonstrate that de-tailed long captions, rather than simple short texts ( e.g. , “a photo of a <class name>” [ 42 ]), can significantly enhance the visual per-ception capabilities of vision-language models (VLMs). To this end, we aim to design a detailed long caption input template, enabling the input text to better guide robust MDE. ## 3.3 Dynamic Caption Encoder 

Although the detailed long caption input contains rich spatial infor-mation, extracting coarse global features ( e.g. , the [CLS] token) as in prior works [ 39 , 42 ] inevitably causes information loss, leading to suboptimal language guidance for robust MDE (see analysis of Tab. 4 in Sec. 4.4.2). To address this, we introduce a dynamic cap-tion encoder which features progressive masked querying blocks to extract fine-grained depth-relevant linguistic representations. Specifically, as illustrated in Fig. 2, given the detailed long cap-tion input 𝐶 , we first extract its CLIP [ 23 , 40 ] features 𝑧 𝐶 ∈ R𝑛 ×𝑑 𝐶 ,where 𝑛 denotes the number of tokens and 𝑑 𝐶 represents the feature dimension of each token. However, not all tokens provide mean-ingful spatial information equally ( e.g. , the model should pay more attention to tokens like “behind” over functional words like “the”). To address this, we leverage a lightweight network consisting of an MLP followed by a sigmoid activation to predict the relevance scores based on 𝑧 𝐶 for the 𝑛 tokens. ## 3.4 Text-Adaptive Decoder 

As illustrated in Fig. 2, following our baseline [ 42 ], the input image 

𝐼 is first processed by a frozen pre-trained variational autoencoder (VAE) [ 14 , 30 ] to obtain its latent representation. Subsequently, text features 𝑄 (𝐿 ) and these image latents are fed into a pre-trained diffusion U-Net [ 30 ] with the timestep set to 𝑡 = 0 [ 42 ]. We extract the hierarchical pyramid features from the up-sampling layers of the diffusion U-Net [ 42 ]. ## 5 Conclusions 

We propose CapDepth, a novel framework leveraging detailed long captions for robust monocular depth estimation (MDE) across both non-Lambertian surfaces and adverse weather conditions via: (1) a detailed long caption input template to provide rich spatial informa-tion, (2) a dynamic caption encoder for fine-grained depth-relevant text feature, (3) a text-adaptive decoder that guides enhanced depth decoding. CapDepth validates that detailed long captions can guide more robust visual perception. However, CapDepth only explores English texts. Future work can investigate generalization across di-verse languages and conduct systematic cross-lingual comparisons to assess language-specific performance variations. ## B.2 More Implementation Details 

Following previous methods [ 39 , 42 ], during the training phase, we incorporate the image augmentation from CutDepth [ 12 ], ran-dom brightness and contrast augmentations within [-0.2, 0.2], and random gamma augmentation within [80, 120], all with a 50% proba-bility. Image inputs are resized to 512 × 512 following priors [ 39 , 42 ]. For the text inputs of CapDepth, we use the InternVL2.5 [ 4] to generate detailed long captions with this prompt: 

Generate descriptions of spatial relationships between all visible objects in the image in the format: {object A} [spatial relationship phrases] {object B}. Ensure that: 1. Spatial relationship phrases only include ‘behind’, ‘in front of’, ‘above’, ‘below’, ‘to the left of’, ‘to the right of’. 2. Nothing unrelated to spatial relationships is output. Example: ‘The dog is in front of and to the right of the mirror. Black cars are behind and below and to the right of the trees.’

## C More Ablation Studies C.1 Varying Text Quality 

We conduct an additional ablation study to evaluate the robust-ness of CapDepth to varying input text quality. Specifically, we use different vision-language models (VLMs) with varying capa-bilities to generate text inputs that meet the requirements of our designed detailed long caption input template. In addition, we intro-duce degradations into the input text to analyze the robustness of CapDepth to descriptions containing incorrect spatial relationships. We also examine the impact on model performance when the input text does not conform to the requirements of the designed detailed long caption input template. As shown in Tab. B, stronger VLMs lead to better model performance in challenging scenarios due to the text inputs they produced that better adhere to the detailed long caption input templaterequirements. When the spatial relationships described in the input text are incorrect, CapDepth still maintains a certain degree of robustness. However, when the input text does not follow the detailed long caption input template, the conveyed spatial information becomes limited, which in turn constrains the robustness of CapDepth in challenging scenarios. These results demonstrate that CapDepth maintains robustness under input texts of varying quality, and further confirm the effectiveness of the proposed detailed long caption input template.
